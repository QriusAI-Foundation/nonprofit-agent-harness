## Programme approach and training content

The programme draws its training material from the ALNAP *State of the Humanitarian System* (SOHS) 2022 edition, which monitors the shape, size and performance of the international humanitarian system over January 2018 to December 2021. The report frames its findings around three questions: what the system is, what it is achieving, and how it is working. The training content therefore centres on the evidence and concepts the report sets out, rather than on any single intervention.

### Core frame of the material

The SOHS is described as a unique, independent longitudinal study that gathers and synthesises evidence to form a picture of the system and assess how well it meets the needs of people affected by crises. Its study team adopts a working definition of the international humanitarian system as the network of interconnected institutional and operational entities through which humanitarian action is undertaken when local and national resources are, on their own, insufficient to meet the needs of a population in crisis.

The report also sets out performance criteria adapted from the OECD-DAC criteria, including sufficiency, coverage, relevance and appropriateness, accountability and participation, effectiveness, efficiency, coherence, complementarity, connectedness and impact.

### Content areas drawn from the report

The training material draws on several thematic areas:

- **Funding and sufficiency.** The report notes that international humanitarian assistance was estimated at $31.3 billion in 2021 and that UN-coordinated appeals were little more than half-funded in 2020, a record low.
- **Reach and targeting.** Only 36% of surveyed aid recipients thought aid went to those who needed it most, and the report discusses how recipient lists are shaped and contested.
- **Relevance and participation.** The report links consultation of affected people with perceived relevance of aid, and identifies feedback loops that are often not closed.
- **Protection and do no harm.** Sexual exploitation and abuse, digital data risks and aid dependency are treated as areas of potential harm requiring attention.
- **Localisation.** The report observes that direct funding to local and national actors remained very low, at about 1.2% of international humanitarian assistance in 2021.

### Limits of the evidence

The report is explicit that persistent data gaps limit definitive assessments of how many people aid reaches, whether humanitarian action saves lives, and how cost-effective responses are. Training participants should therefore treat the figures as the report's own estimates, many of which it cautions should be interpreted carefully.
