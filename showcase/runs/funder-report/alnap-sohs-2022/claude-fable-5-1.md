## Programme approach and training content

**Note on the source.** The document supplied for this section is ALNAP's *The State of the Humanitarian System* (2022 edition). It is a sector-wide performance study rather than a curriculum, and it does not describe a training programme, trainers, participants or numbers reached. This section therefore describes the content and approach of the document as reference material. Any statement about how the organisation delivers it in training would need to be documented separately.

**What the material is.** ALNAP describes itself as a global network of humanitarian organisations dedicated to learning how to improve crisis response. The report is the fifth edition in the series and covers January 2018 to December 2021. It is presented as an independent longitudinal study that synthesises evidence on how well the system meets the needs of people affected by crises.

**Core framing.** The report defines humanitarian action as the principled provision of assistance and protection to save lives, reduce suffering and preserve dignity. Its twelve chapters each answer a key question about the state of the system, and the analysis is organised in three parts. The report opens with an overview of the global context and includes six "Focus on" sections, five on specific global challenges and one on wider networks of support. Performance is assessed against criteria adapted from the OECD-DAC criteria, including sufficiency (whether available resources cover humanitarian needs) and accountability and participation (whether crisis-affected people can influence decisions about assistance and protection).

**Evidence base and methods.** Findings draw on ten research components combining primary data collection and secondary synthesis. These include a survey of 5,487 aid recipients in six crisis contexts conducted by SMS and telephone; a global web survey with 436 completed responses from practitioners and host-government representatives; and country-level focus groups and interviews in Bangladesh (Cox's Bazar), DRC, Ethiopia, Lebanon, Venezuela and Yemen. The evaluation synthesis assessed over 500 humanitarian evaluations, with over 130 selected for in-depth analysis. For the first time, aid recipients in three contexts were consulted on the design of the research itself.

**Stated limitations.** The report is explicit that persistent data gaps limit its ability to give definitive assessments on key questions, including how many people assistance reaches, whether it saves lives and protects people, and how cost-effective responses are. Any training built on this material should carry these caveats forward rather than present the findings as settled.
