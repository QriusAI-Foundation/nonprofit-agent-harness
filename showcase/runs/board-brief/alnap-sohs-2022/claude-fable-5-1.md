# Board brief: The State of the Humanitarian System 2022 (ALNAP)

## What this document is
This is a sector-wide report from ALNAP. It reviews how the international humanitarian system performed from 2018 to 2021. It is not a request for funds and makes no direct ask of our organisation. It is a snapshot we can use to check our own plans against wider trends.

## Six key points

1. **Independent evidence.** The report draws on surveys, interviews, evaluations and financial data. It is the fifth edition in a series that tracks the system over time.

2. **The system is bigger than ever.** Funding reached an estimated $31.3 billion in 2021, nearly double a decade earlier. Growth has slowed, though.

3. **Need is growing faster than money.** In 2020 appeals peaked at $39.3 billion but were little more than half-funded. This was a record low.

4. **Local groups still get little direct money.** Despite promises, local and national NGOs mostly work as sub-contractors with limited say.

5. **Listening to people works.** People who were consulted were far more likely to say the aid they got was enough. This is a cheap, proven lever.

6. **Climate is the top future risk.** Aid workers named climate change as the biggest outside threat facing the system.

## What it asks of us
Nothing formal. But if we are a local or national organisation, the report shows the funding gap we face. If we fund or partner with others, it shows where the sector says we should improve: direct funding, listening to communities, and preparing for climate shocks.

## What the board should decide or watch
- **Decide:** whether to build community consultation into every programme as standard.
- **Decide:** whether to push funders for more direct, flexible support.
- **Watch:** appeal funding levels, which affect every partner we work with.
- **Watch:** climate-related disasters in the places we operate.
- **Watch:** the safety of local staff, who carry most of the risk.

## Evidence cited

1. The report is an independent, long-running study that gathers evidence on how well the humanitarian system meets the needs of people affected by crises.
   > "The SOHS is a unique, independent longitudinal study that gathers and synthesises evidence to form a picture of the system and assess how well it meets the needs of people affected by crises." (Introduction)
2. Humanitarian funding reached an estimated $31.3 billion in 2021, nearly double the level of a decade earlier.
   > "Financially it is larger than ever, reaching an estimated $31.3 billion in 2021 – almost double what it was a decade before." (Chapter 2: What is the shape and size of the humanitarian system? – In brief)
3. Appeals peaked at $39.3 billion in 2020 but were only a little more than half-funded, a record low.
   > "While the COVID-19 pandemic drove a $39.3 billion peak in appeals requirements in 2020, funding rose modestly, meaning the appeals were little more than half-funded – a record low." (Chapter 3: Is there enough aid? – In brief)
4. Local and national NGOs saw no real increase in funding and mostly continued to work as sub-contractors with limited influence.
   > "But there was no real increase in funding for local actors, and for the most part L/NNGOs continued to operate as sub-contractors, with limited influence." (Chapter 9: Does the international system enable local action? – In brief)
5. People who were consulted were much more likely to say the aid they received was enough.
   > "People who felt that they were consulted were 150% more likely to feel that they received enough aid than those who said that they weren’t consulted" (Chapter 3: Is there enough aid? – Views of affected people)
6. Aid workers named climate change as the biggest external threat to the system's future.
   > "Climate change was the external threat most frequently cited by aid practitioners when we asked them about the future of the system (see Chapter 7)." (Is the system fit for the future? – Climate threats)
